What Our Children Built: A Family Guide

ByteBusters National Showcase · Monday 26 October 2026 · Oct 23, 2026 · Olusoji Daniel Ayoola

Your child is one of the young builders presenting at the ByteBusters National Showcase. They built one of 15 projects: 9 games (made with a children's tool called Scratch), 3 AI apps and 3 websites. Every project is about real Nigerian life: money, markets, farming, family, honesty and safety. You already know these things from your work. This guide helps you understand your child's project, so you can talk about it together at home.

How to use this guide at home

Find your child's project in this guide. Read only that part and its word list. It takes about 10 minutes.

  1. Ask your child to show you the project on a phone or laptop, or to explain it with their hands and a sheet of paper.

  2. Ask the 3 big questions: What problem does it solve? How does it work? What did YOU build?

  3. Add your own example. A trader knows prices. A farmer knows harvest and spoilage. A tailor knows measuring and customers. A teacher knows explaining. Tell your child one real story from your work that matches their project. Judges love a real example.

How to help without taking over

  • Let your child talk. Listen first, then ask.

  • If they get stuck, ask "Show me" instead of giving the answer.

  • Praise clear explaining, not big words. A judge must understand them in one try.

  • Each project has its own special words. Help your child use the words of their own project, not words from the other projects.

Part 1 · The Scratch games: the words to know

Scratch is a free tool for making games. Instead of typing, children snap coloured blocks together, like Lego, to give the computer instructions. These words belong to the Scratch games only.

Scratch word What it means Like at home
Sprite A character or object in the game: a person, a jar, a truck An actor in a Nollywood film
Costume A different look for the same sprite: happy, sad, full, empty The same tailor's dummy in different clothes
Stage and backdrop The screen where the game happens, and its background picture The market stall and the cloth behind it
Script A stack of blocks that tells one sprite what to do A recipe card: do this, then this, then this
Green flag The button that starts the game The bell that starts the school day
Variable A box that remembers a number, like money or time left The till book where a trader writes today's sales
If … else A decision: IF this is true, do this, ELSE do that IF it rains, carry an umbrella, ELSE leave it
Repeat (loop) Do the same steps again and again Sweeping: one stroke, again and again, until the floor is clean
Broadcast One sprite "shouts" a message and others react The school bell: everybody hears it and moves
Pick random The computer picks a number by chance Shaking a hand of cards before you deal

Every game was built in small steps: first the title screen, then the boxes and the main loop, then the buttons and their decisions, then the characters, then the ending. Ask your child: "Which step did you build?"

Scratch games at Yewa College, Ilaro

Naira Wise: plan your pocket money

  • The story: Ada gets ₦1,000 pocket money every week for 3 weeks, in ₦200 notes. She puts each note in a jar: SAVE, SPEND or GIVE. She dreams of a ₦1,000 storybook. In week 2, her sandal breaks and the cobbler wants ₦400.

  • How it works: each click on a jar moves ₦200 out of her pocket. Only the SAVE jar puts it in her kolo. If the kolo is too small for the sandal, the money comes from that week's pocket money. At the end, IF the kolo has ₦1,000, Ada buys the book. Save 3 notes a week and she ends with ₦1,400; save only 2 and she has ₦800.

  • The lesson: split money the day you get it, and keep something for emergencies.

  • At home: every trader keeps money aside for bad days, like a sudden repair. Tell your child how you do it.

  • Ask your child: "Why did the sandal week matter so much?" and "What decision does the game make at the end?"

Race Against Rot: store food well, earn more

  • The story: a farmer has 100 tomatoes and an 8-second drive to market. You choose how to store them: open air, a clay pot cooler or sun-drying.

  • How it works: every second on the road, some tomatoes spoil: 8 a second in open air, 3 in the clay pot, 1 when sun-dried (but dried tomatoes sell for half price). At the market: money = tomatoes left x price. Open air earns ₦3,600, the clay pot ₦7,600, sun-drying ₦4,600.

  • The lesson: good storage is money. Food that spoils is hard work thrown away.

  • At home: farmers and market women know this well. Share how you keep produce fresh, or what you lost when you could not.

  • Ask your child: "Why does the clay pot win?" and "Where is the repeat in your game?" (the 8 seconds on the road)

The Great Owambe Budget: enjoy today, plan for Monday

  • The story: plan a party with ₦200,000. Six cards: jollof, canopy, DJ, aso-ebi, decoration and small chops. All six cost ₦270,000, more than the budget. A lender offers ₦50,000, but you must pay back ₦60,000.

  • How it works: each card asks one question: IF the budget is smaller than the price, you cannot buy it; ELSE you pay and the card disappears. Buying jollof, canopy, DJ and small chops uses exactly ₦200,000: a sweet party with no debt. Borrow, and Monday morning comes with the lender.

  • The lesson: plan the party you can pay for. Borrowed money costs more than you borrowed.

  • At home: every family has seen a big party followed by hard months. Talk about how you decide what matters most.

  • Ask your child: "What happens if you click BORROW?" and "How does the game choose the ending?"

Scratch games at Methodist High School, Arigbajo

Honest Scale: honest weight, happy customers

  • The story: you are Mama T, selling rice. 6 customers come, and each one orders some kilos. You put rice on the scale and press SELL.

  • How it works: the customer pays ₦500 for every kilo they asked for. IF you give less than they asked, you keep ₦500 extra, but trust falls by 2. ELSE trust rises by 1. Trust starts at 5. Honest every time, trust reaches 11: Trusted seller! Cheat 3 times, trust falls below 1 and the shop closes.

  • The lesson: cheating earns a little today and loses customers tomorrow.

  • At home: every trader and tailor knows that a good name brings customers back. Tell your child about a seller you stopped buying from, and why.

  • Ask your child: "What does the SELL button decide?" and "Why does trust matter more than ₦500?"

Real vs Fake: check before you buy

  • The story: at Oga Sharp's stall, two products look the same, but one is fake. You are the Fake Hunter. 8 rounds, 3 hearts.

  • How it works: the computer hides the fake on the left or the right by chance, like tossing a coin. Click the fake: +10 points. Click the real one: lose a heart. After each round, a clue card shows how to spot that fake: stones in rice, no expiry date, wrong spelling, no NAFDAC number.

  • The lesson: build a checking habit: seal, NAFDAC number, expiry date and spelling.

  • At home: show your child a real product at home and check its label together. Have you ever been sold something fake?

  • Ask your child: "How does the game hide the fake?" and "What clue would you look for first?"

Family Job Board: help at home, every day

  • The story: Mum calls out chores all day: sweep, fetch water, wash plates. Help Tayo finish as many as he can in 45 seconds, before Daddy comes home.

  • How it works: Mum picks a chore by chance and its spot glows. Click the glowing spot and Tayo does the job. The clock counts down while the sky changes from morning to evening. More than 19 chores wins Gold, more than 11 Silver, otherwise Bronze.

  • The lesson: small jobs done quickly and well add up, and a home works when everyone helps.

  • At home: this one is easy: it is your house! Ask which chore your child would put in the game next.

  • Ask your child: "How does each spot know if Mum called it?" and "How does the game pick the medal?"

Scratch games at Unity High School, Ijoko

Middleman Maze: who really earns from the tomatoes?

  • The story: a farmer sells her tomatoes for ₦10,000. Press SPACE to drive the truck to market. On trip 1, three middlemen each add their cut: ₦5,000, ₦7,000 and ₦8,000. The buyer pays ₦30,000, but the farmer still gets only ₦10,000. On trip 2, the farmers' co-op sells together: the buyer pays ₦25,000 and the farmer gets ₦20,000.

  • How it works: two boxes are always on screen: the Price the buyer pays and what the Farmer Gets. Each middleman adds his cut to the price only. The co-op changes both boxes.

  • The lesson: ask who stands in the middle and what they add. Working together keeps more money for the people who did the work.

  • At home: traders and farmers live this every day. Explain to your child how goods reach your shop or market, and who takes a share on the way.

  • Ask your child: "Why does the price go up but the farmer's money stays the same?"

Shrinking Naira: same money, less food

  • The story: Mum has ₦5,000 every month for 4 months. In month 1 it buys rice, bread, eggs, tomatoes and palm oil exactly. Every month the prices go up, but her money stays the same.

  • How it works: click a food to buy it. IF Mum has less money than the price, it is too expensive. Each new month: rice +₦300, bread +₦200, eggs +₦150, tomatoes +₦150, palm oil +₦100. By month 4, the same 5 foods cost ₦7,700. Press F (or the hand) to freeze the game, so our signing students can explain.

  • The lesson: this is inflation. Money is worth what it can buy, so buy the most important things first.

  • At home: you know this better than anyone. Tell your child what ₦5,000 bought last year and what it buys today.

  • Ask your child: "What does the NEXT MONTH button change?"

The Empty Barn: catch it early, save the farm

  • The story: 6 animals live in the barn: goats, chickens, a sheep and a duck. One by one, they show early signs of sickness. Click them fast to treat them. Wait too long and they get very sick; wait longer and they are lost. You have 3 vaccines and 45 seconds until sunset.

  • How it works: each animal waits a random time, then shows early signs. Treat it at early signs: +20 points. Treat it when very sick: only +10. A vaccine heals every sick animal at once. At sunset, more than 4 animals left means the farm is strong.

  • The lesson: act on early signs. A small problem caught early is cheap to fix.

  • At home: farmers know a sick animal can infect the rest. Tell your child how you spot a sick animal, or a sick plant.

  • Ask your child: "Why are early signs worth more points?"

Part 2 · The AI apps: the words to know

The AI team at Yewa College built 3 apps that use artificial intelligence (AI): a computer program that has read a huge amount of writing and can write answers in plain language. Our apps use an AI from a company called OpenAI. These words belong to the AI apps only.

The easiest way to understand an AI app is a restaurant:

In the restaurant In our AI app
The customer orders The user types into the form on the page
The waiter carries the order The page sends the order to the server
The kitchen checks the order The server checks it, removes private details and adds our rules
The chef cooks The AI model writes the answer
The waiter brings the food The page shows the answer
AI word What it means Like at home
AI model The "brain" that writes the answer. We use one called GPT-6 Luna A very well-read assistant who answers anything you ask
Prompt or instructions Our written rules that the AI reads every time, for example "use simple English" A teacher's marking guide
Input The user's own words and choices from the form The customer's order
Output The answer the AI writes back The finished dress handed to the customer
Guardrails Safety rules the AI must never break, checked again by our code The fence around a farm
Privacy filter Our code deletes names, ages and phone numbers before the AI sees them Covering your PIN at the ATM
API key A secret password that lets our app use the AI. It is paid for by an adult and kept hidden on the server The key to the shop's cash box: only the owner holds it
Demo mode When there is no internet or no key, the app shows a sample answer and says so honestly A display sample in a shop window, labelled "sample"
Vibe coding We told an AI helper called Claude, step by step, what to build. It wrote the code; we tested it and must explain it Telling a skilled carpenter exactly what table you want, then checking every leg

The one sentence every AI student should be able to say: "The AI reads our rules plus the user's words together, every time, and writes one answer. Our code checks the order before, and the answer after."

The 3 AI apps (Yewa College)

Inflation Explainer AI: why did the price go up?

  • The problem: prices keep rising, and many young people do not understand why.

  • How it works: the user types an item, its old price and year, and its new price and year. Our code does the maths first: bread from ₦300 in 2020 to ₦350 in 2024 is a 16.7% rise. Then the AI explains in simple words: what happened, why prices rise (with a Nigerian example like garri or transport), and one tip.

  • Why our code does the maths, not the AI: AI can make maths mistakes. Code is exact every time. The AI's job is the explaining.

  • At home: give your child a real example from your shop or market, with real old and new prices, and try it together.

  • Ask your child: "What does the AI receive?" (our rules plus the item, the prices and the percentage our code worked out)

Community Voice AI: turn a complaint into a proper letter

  • The problem: many people have a real community problem (a broken tap, a bad road, rubbish) but do not know how to write a polite, formal letter about it.

  • How it works: the user chooses who the letter is for (the school principal, a community leader, the local government office, or a neighbour or landlord), picks a tone (polite, or firm but respectful), describes the problem and says what they want. The AI writes a short formal letter of under 150 words. A copy button copies it.

  • Its guardrails: the AI must never invent names, dates or facts. It leaves spaces like [Your Name] for the user to fill in. It must never be rude or threatening. IF the problem sounds dangerous, it adds a line telling the student to tell a trusted adult.

  • At home: teachers, traders and parents write letters to schools, landlords and leaders. Show your child a letter you wrote, or tell them a problem in your area to try.

  • Ask your child: "Why does the AI leave [Your Name] empty?"

Naija Career Compass AI: what could I become?

  • The problem: many students know only a few careers, and career advice often does not match what they actually enjoy.

  • How it works: the student answers 9 short questions: favourite subject, what they enjoy, what makes them lose track of time, what they are curious about, what comes naturally, a favourite game, how far they want to study, where they love to work, and anything else. The AI suggests exactly 3 careers, each with a picture, the reason it fits, the subjects to focus on, real Nigerian routes (WAEC/NECO, JAMB, polytechnic, technical college, apprenticeship, NABTEB) and one small thing to try this week. No name or age is asked.

  • Its guardrails: at least one career must go through university and at least one must be a skilled trade or hands-on job. The AI must never say a career is not for you because of who you are. Our code checks these rules, and if the AI breaks one, it asks again.

  • At home: this is where your work matters most. A tailor, mechanic, nurse or teacher can tell the child honestly how they learned their trade. That is a real Nigerian route.

  • Ask your child: "How does the app protect the user's privacy?" and "Why must one career be a trade?"

Part 3 · The websites: the words to know

The Web team at Yewa College built 3 websites: pages you open in a browser on a phone or laptop. A website is like a building: HTML builds the rooms, CSS paints and decorates them, and JavaScript brings the electricity that makes things work when you press a switch. These words belong to the websites only.

Website word What it means Like at home
Browser The app you use to open websites, like Chrome The door you walk through to enter a shop
HTML The code that builds the parts of a page: titles, text, buttons, pictures The walls and rooms of a house
CSS The code that gives the page its colours, sizes and layout The paint, curtains and furniture
JavaScript The code that makes the page react when you click or type The electricity and switches
Data The facts the page shows, stored as a list, like the crops or the trades The price list on a shop wall
Function A named set of steps the page can run again and again, like "show the calendar" A recipe you cook every Sunday
Button and click When you click a button, the JavaScript runs the steps for it Ringing the bell on a shop counter
Responsive The page changes its layout to fit a small phone or a big screen A tailor adjusting one design for every size
Offline The site works with no internet once it is on your device A printed book: no data needed
Hosting and link Putting the site online (we use GitHub) so anyone can open it with a link Renting a shop space with an address people can find

The Web team planned each page themselves and used an AI coding helper to type parts of the code faster, then tested and explained every part. Ask your child: "Which part is HTML, which part is CSS, and which part is JavaScript?"

The 3 websites (Yewa College)

Scam Shield: think before you click

  • The problem: young people get fake messages every day ("You won ₦2.5 million!", "Your account will be blocked, send your PIN") and do not always see the warning signs.

  • How it works: the site has 6 pages, on a top menu (laptop) or a bottom menu (phone):

    • Home: an animation of a scammer shooting an arrow marked SCAM, blocked by our shield, plus what to do if you suspect a scam.

    • Scam Types: 8 common scams (fake prizes, fake jobs, fake sellers, romance and more), with examples.

    • Spot It: the user clicks the suspicious words in a made-up message, then sees which red flags they found or missed.

    • Verify: paste a doubtful message and the page lists its warning signs and the closest made-up example. A report can be saved on that phone only.

    • Q&A and a 10-question quiz that earns points and badges.

  • Good to know: every message, name and number in it is made up. It works offline, with no data. It is a learning tool, not a police or bank system.

  • The lesson: stop, don't share your PIN or code, check with someone you trust, and report it.

  • At home: read a suspicious SMS or WhatsApp message you received with your child, and find the warning signs together.

  • Ask your child: "How does the Verify page decide the warning signs?"

Harvest Calendar Naija: when to plant, when to harvest

  • The problem: planting or harvesting at the wrong time wastes seeds, money and food.

  • How it works: the page shows 6 crops (maize, yam, cassava, tomato, okra and ofada rice) across the 12 months. Green squares mean plant, gold means harvest. The page reads today's month from the computer and outlines it. Click a crop's name for a farming tip. The Play the year button moves through the months one by one, like a film.

  • The lesson: farming follows the seasons. Good timing means a better harvest.

  • At home: farmers can check the calendar against their own experience. Is it right for your area? Your child can add your advice as a tip.

  • Ask your child: "Where is the list of crops kept, and how does the page decide the colour of each square?"

Apprentice Pathway Explorer: learn a trade, build a future

  • The problem: many young people do not know which trades they could learn, or how to start an apprenticeship.

  • How it works: the page shows 6 trades as cards: electrician, auto mechanic, tailor or fashion designer, hairdresser or barber, baker and caterer. Buttons show one group at a time (Technical, Creative or Food). A search box finds a trade by a word (try tailor, wiring or bread). If nothing matches, it says No trade found.

  • Next idea: the team plans to add an AI helper that answers questions about trades.

  • The lesson: a skilled trade is a real, respected path to a good living.

  • At home: if you are a tailor, mechanic, hairdresser, baker or caterer, tell your child how you learned, how long it took and what it costs to start. That is real information for their presentation.

  • Ask your child: "What happens in the code when I type a word in the search box?"

Showcase day: Monday 26 October

Each team presents its project to judges and visitors in a short talk. Every child has a speaking part: one opens, one shows how it works, others explain the parts they built, and one closes. Some children sign their part, with a partner pointing at the screen.

Children will say honestly: "Our trainer prepared the starter. We learned how every part works, we tested it, and we changed it ourselves." This is true and it is good: learning from a starter is how professionals learn too.

How families can help before Monday

  • Listen to your child's speaking part once a day, out loud. It is about 45 seconds.

  • Give one real example from your work that your child can use. Judges remember real stories.

  • Ask the 3 big questions: What problem does it solve? How does it work? What did YOU build?

  • Help your child sleep well on Sunday night and arrive early on Monday.

If you have questions, speak to the ByteBusters centre lead at your child's school.
